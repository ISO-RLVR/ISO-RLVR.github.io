From spectral inheritance to Isospectral Optimization. Unconstrained RLVR changes both singular frames while the weight spectra stay close to their base values — and restoring the base spectrum Σ0 preserves most acquired gains. ISO turns this regularity into an inductive bias: reuse Σ0, optimize (U, V) — offline as ISO-Merger and online as ISO-Optimizer.

TL;DR RLVR can reuse the base model's singular-value spectra while acquiring new capabilities by adapting the singular frames. We verify this functionally (restoring the base spectrum preserves most acquired gains, and fixing it throughout training supports strong learning), and turn it into ISO, a fixed-spectrum optimization stack: ISO-Merger composes shared-base RL experts with no data, rollouts, gradients, or distillation, and ISO-Optimizer runs AdamW/Muon on the frames and reaches matched RLVR accuracy with 1.5× fewer actor updates on Qwen3-4B-Base.
Observation → Function

Spectra stay: spectral inheritance in RLVR

Write each weight matrix as W = U Σ V⊤: the spectrum Σ sets the scale of each singular mode, the frames (U, V) set its output/input directions. Across RLVR runs — including a public reasoning run trained for 3,000+ updates — the learned checkpoint sits strikingly close to the fixed-spectrum family of its base weights: the pooled spectral residual is approximately 3% of the total base-to-RL weight displacement. In the sequential SFT and RLVR comparison, the mean relative residuals are approximately 32% and 2%, respectively. These transitions start from different checkpoints; the SFT endpoint is the RLVR base.

Dimension-normalized spectrum-changing energy remains order-one relative to an isotropic reference for RLVR, so proximity alone does not establish a strong preference for spectrum-preserving updates. We test spectral reuse functionally: restore the base spectrum after training, or never let it move at all.

Takeaway 1 — Spectral inheritance. RLVR can reuse the base model's weight spectra rather than rewrite them: restoring the base spectrum in the RL-trained frames preserves most acquired gains, and training with the spectrum frozen from step one still learns strongly. These interventions support reusing the base spectrum while adapting the frames.

Structure

Frames move — and both must stay adaptable

If the spectrum can be inherited, which variables must remain free? We reconstruct RL endpoints under four structural restrictions and measure how much of the checkpoint update each one fails to explain (two-stage VLA RL pipeline, transition W0 → W2, median across layers):

Remix within both incoming subspaces (umix) 87%
Freeze output subspace only (uL) 45%
Freeze input subspace only (uR) 42%
Fix the spectrum, adapt both frames (uiso) 1.8%

Fraction of the checkpoint update left unexplained by the best reconstruction in each class (lower is better; 100% = no change explained). The same pattern recurs across sequential RL stages with distinct objectives.

Takeaway 2 — Fix the spectrum, keep both frames adaptable. Even permissive remixing inside the incoming subspaces, or freezing just one side, leaves a large share of the update unexplained. Retaining the incoming spectrum while adapting both frames leaves only ~2% — directly motivating ISO's parameterization W(U,V) = U Σ0 V⊤.
Method

ISO: Isospectral Optimization

Inherit the spectrum, optimize the frames:  W(U, V) = U Σ0 V⊤,  U, V on the Stiefel manifold.

U — output frame · moves

Σ0 — base spectrum · frozen

V⊤ — input frame · moves

The base optimizer receives ambient factor gradients and takes a tentative frame update that need not lie in the tangent plane. Polar restoration restores orthonormal frames, so Wt = Ut Σ0 Vt⊤ retains the base spectrum in exact arithmetic. The ellipsoids and update paths are schematic; the gray planes show local tangent geometry.

Every represented matrix shares the base spectrum Σ0 exactly; all learning and composition happen in the frame coordinates, with a polar retraction restoring orthonormality. ISO is a stack, not a single optimizer — one principle, two instantiations spanning the RLVR workflow:

Offline

ISO-Merger — compose RL experts without rollouts

Take specialists trained from a shared base, express each expert as a displacement of the singular frames (project → mask trailing modes → solve unit-retention coefficients → retract), and rebuild one fixed-spectrum model.

No post-merge data, rollouts, gradient updates, or on-policy distillation.
Online

ISO-Optimizer — fixed-spectrum RLVR training

Apply your existing optimizer — AdamW or Muon, states and all — to (U, V) instead of W, keep Σ0 frozen, and retract after each step — a strictly more constrained model class.

Fewer training steps, higher rewards.

Results · Online

ISO-Optimizer: same reward signal, fewer steps, higher accuracy

ISO-AdamW uses a frame learning rate of 1×10−6 at both math scales, while AdamW and Muon baselines are tuned over learning-rate sweeps. At 400 actor updates, ISO-AdamW achieves 29.41 on Qwen3-1.7B-Base and 46.98 on Qwen3-4B-Base — improvements of 1.21 and 2.16 points over the strongest tested AdamW baselines.

Qwen3-4B-Base, DeepMath-103K. ISO-AdamW reaches AdamW's 400-update accuracy at update 260, saving 124 hours (34%), and finishes 2.16 points higher at update 400. Additional optimizer computation is about 49 seconds per step (1.2%) of the profiled end-to-end step time.

Comparison with Pion

Pion is a concurrent spectrum-preserving optimizer with its own momentum and step-size rules. ISO applies a chosen base optimizer directly to both singular frames. Among completed 400-update runs, ISO-AdamW achieves the highest final accuracy at both scales; Pion outperforms AdamW at 10−6 but not the strongest tested AdamW configuration.

Qwen3-1.7B-Base (left) and Qwen3-4B-Base (right). On 4B, ISO-AdamW reaches Pion's 400-update accuracy using approximately 40% as many actor updates.

Pion uses its official implementation. Learning rates are swept over {1, 2, 3}×10−6 on 1.7B; the 3×10−6 run is stopped early after accuracy starts declining at update 160. On 4B, computational constraints limit Pion to its published RLVR learning rate of 10−6, without further tuning.

Ablation: does learning the spectrum help?

We compare ISO-AdamW with a variant that also trains the diagonal spectrum Σ, initialized at Σ0, while retaining the same factorization and orthonormal frame constraints. On Qwen3-1.7B-Base, fixed and trainable spectra yield similar learning trajectories.

Fixed vs. trainable spectrum. At update 400, aggregate accuracy is 29.4 vs. 29.6, respectively. The learned spectrum's relative drift is only 0.0065%. Comparable observed accuracy supports reusing the base spectrum.
